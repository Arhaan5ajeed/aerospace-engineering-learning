## Week Overview

### What are we studying?

The aerospace industry is commonly divided into three broad sectors:

1. Civil aviation.
2. Defence aerospace.
3. Space industry.

Although these sectors share engineering disciplines, technologies and suppliers, they operate under different requirements.

For example, consider three aircraft or aerospace systems:

- A commercial passenger aircraft.
- A military fighter aircraft.
- A satellite launch vehicle.

All three require structures, propulsion, materials, electronics, software and extensive testing.

However, their design objectives are different.

A commercial aircraft must transport passengers safely and economically over thousands of flights.

A fighter aircraft must fulfil military missions under demanding operational conditions.

A launch vehicle must deliver its payload into a specified trajectory while operating under extreme acceleration, vibration, thermal and pressure conditions.

The same engineering discipline can therefore have different priorities depending on the sector.

### The three sectors at a glance

| Dimension | Civil aviation | Defence aerospace | Space industry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary purpose | Transportation | Military capability | Access to and utilisation of space |
| Primary customers | Airlines and civil operators | Governments and militaries | Governments, businesses and scientific organisations |
| Main objective | Safe and efficient operation | Mission effectiveness | Successful launch, operation and delivery of space services |
| Operating environment | Earth's atmosphere | Atmospheric and sometimes space environments | Space and launch environments |
| Key requirement | Airworthiness and safety | Mission capability and operational readiness | Mission success and spacecraft reliability |
| Acquisition | Commercial purchase or leasing | Government procurement | Government, commercial or institutional procurement |
| Regulatory focus | Aviation safety and certification | Military requirements, security and qualification | Launch licensing, space law and international obligations |
| Lifecycle | Often decades of operation | Long-term military service and support | Mission-dependent, often difficult to repair after deployment |

### An important distinction

These sectors are not completely isolated.

A military transport aircraft may use technologies developed for commercial aviation.

A commercial satellite may use technology originally developed for defence applications.

A space launch company may develop propulsion technologies that have applications in other aerospace sectors.

Companies can also operate across multiple sectors.

The distinction is therefore based on the product's intended application, customer, operating environment and regulatory framework rather than simply the technology used.

## Day 1 — Understanding the Three Aerospace Sectors

> **Objective:** Establish a clear conceptual understanding of civil aviation, defence aerospace and the space industry, including their purposes, customers, products and operating environments.

### 1.1 Civil aviation

Civil aviation refers to aviation activities conducted for non-military purposes.

It includes:

- Commercial passenger transportation.
- Cargo transportation.
- Business aviation.
- General aviation.
- Civil helicopters.
- Agricultural aviation.
- Aerial surveying.
- Search and rescue operations.
- Civil flight training.

Its central purpose is to enable safe and efficient aircraft operations.

#### Who are the customers?

Civil aviation customers include:

- Airlines.
- Cargo operators.
- Private aircraft operators.
- Aircraft leasing companies.
- Business aviation companies.
- Government civil aviation organisations.
- Passengers and cargo customers.

#### What products and services are involved?

- Commercial aircraft.
- Regional aircraft.
- Business jets.
- Helicopters.
- Aircraft engines.
- Avionics.
- Airport infrastructure.
- Air traffic management.
- Aircraft maintenance.
- Flight operations software.

#### What defines success?

Civil aviation success is measured through factors such as:

- Safety.
- Reliability.
- Operational availability.
- Fuel efficiency.
- Passenger comfort.
- Operating costs.
- Schedule reliability.
- Regulatory compliance.
- Commercial profitability.

An aircraft that performs exceptionally well but is too expensive to operate may not be commercially successful.

### 1.2 Defence aerospace

Defence aerospace involves the development, acquisition and operation of aerospace systems for military and national security purposes.

It includes:

- Fighter aircraft.
- Military transport aircraft.
- Helicopters.
- Unmanned aerial vehicles.
- Surveillance aircraft.
- Military satellites.
- Missiles.
- Radar systems.
- Electronic warfare systems.
- Military communication systems.

#### Who are the customers?

The primary customers are:

- Governments.
- Defence ministries.
- Armed forces.
- Military procurement agencies.
- Government defence organisations.

#### What products and services are involved?

- Combat aircraft.
- Military engines.
- Weapon systems.
- Radar and surveillance systems.
- Navigation systems.
- Military communications.
- Autonomous platforms.
- Command and control software.
- Simulation and training systems.
- Maintenance and logistics services.

#### What defines success?

Defence aerospace success is evaluated through:

- Mission effectiveness.
- Reliability.
- Survivability.
- Operational availability.
- Sensor and weapon performance.
- Interoperability.
- Security.
- Maintainability.
- Strategic relevance.

Commercial profitability still matters to defence contractors, but the military customer's primary concern is whether the acquired system fulfils its operational requirements.

### 1.3 The space industry

The space industry develops, launches, operates and commercialises technologies and services associated with outer space.

It includes:

- Launch vehicles.
- Satellites.
- Spacecraft.
- Space stations.
- Ground stations.
- Satellite communication.
- Earth observation.
- Navigation services.
- Space science missions.
- Space exploration.
- Space situational awareness.
- In-space transportation.

#### Who are the customers?

Space industry customers include:

- National space agencies.
- Governments.
- Defence organisations.
- Telecommunications companies.
- Commercial satellite operators.
- Scientific institutions.
- Earth observation companies.
- Research organisations.

#### What products and services are involved?

- Launch services.
- Satellite manufacturing.
- Satellite communication.
- Earth observation data.
- Navigation services.
- Spacecraft components.
- Ground infrastructure.
- Satellite operations.
- Space-based analytics.

#### What defines success?

Space industry success depends on:

- Successful launch.
- Accurate orbital insertion.
- Mission completion.
- Spacecraft reliability.
- Payload performance.
- Communication availability.
- Data quality.
- Mission lifetime.
- Regulatory compliance.
- Commercial viability.

A satellite may be successfully manufactured but still represent a failed mission if it cannot reach its intended orbit or establish communication with its ground segment.

### 1.4 Why the sectors have different engineering priorities

Consider the design of three propulsion systems.

#### Commercial turbofan engine

The primary objectives include:

- Fuel efficiency.
- Reliability.
- Low emissions.
- Noise reduction.
- Maintenance cost.
- Long operational life.

#### Military fighter engine

The objectives may include:

- High thrust-to-weight ratio.
- Acceleration.
- Performance across a wide flight envelope.
- Thermal management.
- Reliability.
- Operational robustness.
- Integration with military aircraft requirements.

#### Rocket engine

The objectives may include:

- Required thrust.
- Specific impulse.
- Propellant efficiency.
- Structural integrity.
- Combustion stability.
- Thermal protection.
- Reliable operation during the launch sequence.

The fundamental physics of propulsion remains relevant in all three cases.

However, the engineering optimisation problem changes because the intended mission changes.

### 1.5 Sector boundaries are not absolute

Some technologies and products serve more than one sector.

Examples:

- GNSS navigation supports civilian aircraft, military platforms and spacecraft.
- Satellite communication serves commercial, government and military customers.
- Composite materials are used in passenger aircraft, fighter aircraft and launch vehicles.
- Autonomous systems are developed for UAVs, spacecraft and civil aircraft.
- Radar technology can have both civilian and military applications.

These are called dual-use technologies when they have both civilian and military applications.

The same technology may be subject to different export controls, security restrictions or certification requirements depending on its application.

# Day 2 — Civil Aviation: Safety, Airworthiness and Commercial Operations

> **Objective:** Understand the civil aviation industry's safety-first philosophy, commercial operating model, airworthiness requirements, certification processes and passenger transportation ecosystem.

## 2.1 What makes civil aviation different?

Civil aviation operates in a highly regulated environment where safety is a fundamental requirement.

Commercial aircraft transport large numbers of passengers and operate repeatedly across national and international airspace.

An aircraft may perform several flights each day and remain in service for decades.

Therefore, the industry requires systems that can operate reliably over long periods while satisfying strict safety requirements.

Civil aviation is not simply about designing an aircraft that can fly.

It is about designing an aircraft that can operate safely, consistently and economically throughout its service life.

## 2.2 Safety

Safety refers to the condition in which risks are reduced and controlled to an acceptable level.

In aviation, safety cannot be established through a single inspection or test.

It requires a continuous system involving:

- Safe aircraft design.
- Manufacturing quality.
- Maintenance.
- Pilot training.
- Operational procedures.
- Air traffic management.
- Airport infrastructure.
- Safety reporting.
- Risk assessment.
- Regulatory oversight.

### Why is aviation safety so demanding?

Aircraft operate in an environment where failures can have serious consequences.

Potential hazards include:

- Engine failure.
- Structural fatigue.
- Loss of hydraulic pressure.
- Electrical failures.
- Sensor malfunctions.
- Software faults.
- Bird strikes.
- Severe weather.
- Human error.
- Maintenance errors.

Engineers must identify possible failure conditions and establish appropriate prevention, detection and mitigation measures.

### Reliability and safety

Reliability and safety are related but not identical.

**Reliability** refers to the probability that a system performs its intended function without failure for a specified period under specified conditions.

**Safety** concerns whether risks associated with the system are acceptably controlled.

A highly reliable system can still have unsafe failure modes.

For example, a system may fail infrequently but have a dangerous failure mode when it does fail.

Therefore, aerospace engineers consider both reliability and the consequences of failure.

## 2.3 Safety management systems

A Safety Management System (SMS) is an organised framework for managing safety risks.

It typically involves:

1. Safety policy and objectives.
2. Safety risk management.
3. Safety assurance.
4. Safety promotion.

An SMS helps organisations identify hazards, assess risks, monitor performance and improve safety practices.

SMS principles are used across different aviation organisations, including operators, maintenance organisations and other aviation service providers.

### Hazard versus risk

These terms should not be confused.

**Hazard:** A condition or object with the potential to cause harm.

**Risk:** The assessment of the likelihood and severity of the consequences associated with a hazard.

For example:

- Hazard: A damaged aircraft component.
- Potential consequence: Component failure during operation.
- Risk: Determined by evaluating the probability and severity of the resulting failure.

## 2.4 Airworthiness

Airworthiness is one of the most important concepts in civil aviation.

It refers to the condition of an aircraft or aeronautical product being safe and suitable for operation in accordance with applicable requirements.

An aircraft must satisfy the relevant airworthiness standards before it can enter service.

However, airworthiness is not a one-time achievement.

It must be maintained throughout the aircraft's operational life.

### Initial airworthiness

Initial airworthiness concerns the aircraft's design and production compliance before it enters service.

It involves:

- Approved design.
- Structural integrity.
- System safety.
- Performance requirements.
- Manufacturing conformity.
- Required testing.
- Certification.

### Continuing airworthiness

Continuing airworthiness concerns maintaining the aircraft in a condition that remains compliant with applicable requirements throughout its operational life.

It involves:

- Scheduled maintenance.
- Inspections.
- Repairs.
- Modifications.
- Airworthiness directives.
- Component replacement.
- Maintenance records.
- Configuration control.

### Example

Suppose an aircraft is certified and enters commercial service.

After several years, an inspection identifies fatigue cracking in a structural component.

The operator must follow the applicable approved maintenance and airworthiness procedures.

Depending on the findings, this may involve inspection, repair, replacement or other corrective action.

The fact that the aircraft was originally certified does not remove the need to maintain its airworthiness.

## 2.5 Aircraft certification

Certification is the formal process of demonstrating that an aircraft or aeronautical product complies with applicable requirements.

Certification is not simply a manufacturer's declaration that its aircraft is safe.

It involves evidence, evaluation and approval by the relevant authority.

### Major certification activities

- Requirements definition.
- Design evaluation.
- Structural analysis.
- Ground testing.
- Flight testing.
- System safety assessment.
- Software and hardware assurance.
- Production conformity.
- Documentation.
- Regulatory review.

### Important certification terminology

**Type Certificate**

Approval of an aircraft type design that has demonstrated compliance with applicable airworthiness standards.

**Production Approval**

Approval or acceptance of the production system under the applicable regulatory framework.

**Certificate of Airworthiness**

A document issued or recognised by the relevant authority for an individual aircraft that establishes its airworthiness status under the applicable framework.

**Supplemental Type Certificate**

An approval for a modification to an aircraft's type design.

These terms are related but do not represent the same approval.

## 2.6 Commercial aircraft operations

Civil aviation is also a commercial business.

Airlines acquire aircraft to generate revenue through passenger and cargo transportation.

Their business performance depends on several variables.

### Revenue factors

- Passenger demand.
- Ticket prices.
- Cargo demand.
- Route selection.
- Aircraft capacity.
- Load factor.
- Additional services.

### Cost factors

- Fuel.
- Aircraft leasing or financing.
- Maintenance.
- Airport charges.
- Personnel.
- Insurance.
- Navigation charges.
- Spare parts.
- Ground operations.

### Load factor

Load factor measures how much of an airline's available passenger or cargo capacity is utilised.

For passenger airlines, it is commonly expressed as:

$$
\text{Load Factor}=\frac{\text{Revenue Passenger Kilometres}}{\text{Available Seat Kilometres}}\times 100
$$

A higher load factor generally indicates better utilisation of available seating capacity, although profitability depends on other revenue and cost factors as well.

### Why does aircraft efficiency matter?

Fuel represents a major operating expense for many airlines.

An aircraft with improved fuel efficiency can reduce operating costs over thousands of flight hours.

This is one reason why aircraft OEMs continuously invest in:

- Aerodynamic improvements.
- Engine efficiency.
- Lightweight materials.
- Improved flight management systems.
- Reduced maintenance requirements.

## 2.7 Passengers and the human factor

Civil aviation is designed around the safe transportation of people and cargo.

Passenger considerations include:

- Cabin safety.
- Emergency evacuation.
- Cabin pressure.
- Temperature control.
- Noise.
- Accessibility.
- Passenger comfort.
- Human factors.

Human factors engineering studies how people interact with machines, systems and procedures.

It is important because aircraft operation involves pilots, cabin crew, air traffic controllers, maintenance personnel and ground staff.

A technically excellent aircraft must also be operable by trained humans under realistic conditions.

## 2.8 Major civil aviation regulators

Examples include:

| Organisation | Role |
|---|---|
| FAA | United States civil aviation regulator |
| EASA | European Union aviation safety agency |
| DGCA | India's Directorate General of Civil Aviation |
| UK CAA | United Kingdom Civil Aviation Authority |

The International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) is a specialised agency of the United Nations.

ICAO develops international Standards and Recommended Practices for civil aviation.

It is important to distinguish ICAO from national regulators.

ICAO establishes an international framework, while national authorities implement and enforce applicable requirements within their jurisdictions.

# Day 3 — Defence Aerospace: Mission Capability and Military Requirements

> **Objective:** Understand how military aerospace systems are developed around operational requirements, mission effectiveness, security, qualification, procurement and lifecycle support.

## 3.1 What makes defence aerospace different?

Defence aerospace is designed around military requirements.

A military aircraft is not necessarily designed to transport passengers economically or maximise commercial profitability.

Instead, it is designed to fulfil specific missions.

Examples include:

- Air superiority.
- Ground attack.
- Surveillance.
- Reconnaissance.
- Air transport.
- Search and rescue.
- Maritime patrol.
- Electronic warfare.
- Intelligence gathering.
- Military logistics.

The aircraft's design is therefore influenced by the operational environment and the mission it must perform.

## 3.2 Mission capability

Mission capability refers to the ability of a system to perform its intended military role.

Consider a hypothetical military surveillance aircraft.

Its mission may require it to:

- Remain airborne for extended periods.
- Collect information using sensors.
- Operate in difficult environmental conditions.
- Communicate with command centres.
- Process and transmit mission data.
- Maintain reliable operation during demanding missions.

The aircraft's performance is evaluated against these operational requirements.

### Mission requirements versus general performance

An aircraft may have excellent speed and manoeuvrability but still be unsuitable for a surveillance mission if it lacks the required sensors, endurance or communication capabilities.

Therefore, military performance must be evaluated in the context of the intended mission.

## 3.3 Military requirements

Military requirements are translated into technical and operational specifications.

These may include:

### Performance requirements

- Maximum speed.
- Operating altitude.
- Range.
- Endurance.
- Payload.
- Manoeuvrability.
- Climb performance.

### Mission requirements

- Sensor capability.
- Navigation.
- Communication.
- Mission equipment.
- Data processing.
- Interoperability.

### Environmental requirements

- Temperature.
- Humidity.
- Vibration.
- Shock.
- Dust.
- Rain.
- Electromagnetic interference.
- Corrosion.

### Operational requirements

- Reliability.
- Maintainability.
- Availability.
- Deployability.
- Training.
- Logistics.
- Support infrastructure.

A military system must satisfy a combination of technical and operational requirements rather than one isolated performance metric.

## 3.4 Survivability and robustness

Military aerospace systems may operate in environments where they face hostile conditions and threats.

Survivability refers to the ability of a system and its mission to withstand or manage threats and adverse conditions.

It can involve:

- Structural robustness.
- Redundancy.
- Damage tolerance.
- Electronic protection.
- Cybersecurity.
- Signature management.
- Secure communication.
- Fault detection and recovery.

The specific requirements depend on the mission and platform.

### Redundancy

Redundancy means providing additional components or pathways so that a system can continue operating if one element fails.

For example, an aircraft may use multiple independent systems for critical functions.

Redundancy improves resilience, but it also increases:

- Weight.
- Cost.
- Complexity.
- Maintenance requirements.

Engineers must balance the benefits of redundancy against its disadvantages.

## 3.5 Security and classification

Defence programmes may involve sensitive or classified information.

This information may include:

- System architecture.
- Sensor capabilities.
- Performance specifications.
- Operational procedures.
- Mission data.
- Software.
- Communications.
- Technical documentation.

Access to sensitive information is controlled according to applicable national laws and security requirements.

### Why does classification matter?

A defence system may contain technologies or information that could provide an advantage to an adversary if disclosed.

Therefore, organisations may implement:

- Personnel security checks.
- Access controls.
- Secure communication.
- Classified facilities.
- Information handling procedures.
- Cybersecurity controls.
- Export restrictions.

### Classification versus export control

These are not identical.

**Classification** concerns the protection of information based on its sensitivity and potential security impact.

**Export control** concerns restrictions on transferring specified goods, software, technology or technical information across borders.

An item may be export-controlled without being classified.

Likewise, classified information may be subject to additional restrictions beyond ordinary export controls.

## 3.6 Qualification

Qualification is the process of demonstrating that a product, component or system satisfies specified technical, environmental and performance requirements for its intended use.

In defence aerospace, qualification may involve testing under demanding conditions.

Examples include:

- Temperature testing.
- Vibration testing.
- Shock testing.
- Humidity testing.
- Electromagnetic compatibility testing.
- Environmental exposure testing.
- Performance testing.
- Reliability assessment.

### Why is qualification important?

A component may work perfectly in a laboratory but fail when exposed to vibration, temperature changes or other operational conditions.

Qualification provides evidence that the component can satisfy the specified requirements in its intended environment.

### Certification versus qualification

These terms should not be used interchangeably.

**Certification** generally refers to formal approval or recognition that applicable requirements have been satisfied under a defined regulatory or approval framework.

**Qualification** demonstrates that a product or system meets specified technical requirements for its intended application.

A qualified component is not automatically certified for every application.

Likewise, certification does not eliminate the need for ongoing qualification, verification or testing where required.

## 3.7 Procurement

Defence aerospace acquisition is generally organised through government procurement.

The military identifies a capability requirement.

The government and relevant procurement authorities determine how the requirement will be fulfilled.

Possible acquisition routes include:

- Indigenous development.
- Domestic procurement.
- Foreign procurement.
- Government-to-government arrangements.
- Licensed production.
- Collaborative development.
- Private-sector contracting.

### Why are defence procurement cycles long?

Major military systems involve:

- Complex technical requirements.
- Large financial commitments.
- Security assessments.
- Testing and evaluation.
- Contract negotiations.
- Industrial participation.
- Technology transfer considerations.
- Long-term maintenance planning.

The procurement process can therefore extend over many years.

## 3.8 Lifecycle support

A military aircraft is not useful simply because it has been delivered.

It must remain available for military operations.

Lifecycle support includes:

- Maintenance.
- Spare parts.
- Repair.
- Overhaul.
- Technical documentation.
- Training.
- Software updates.
- Equipment upgrades.
- Logistics.
- Ground support equipment.

### Operational availability

Operational availability refers broadly to the proportion of time a system is available for its intended operational use.

A military aircraft with excellent technical specifications but frequent maintenance problems may provide limited operational value.

For example, an aircraft fleet may contain 100 aircraft, but only a fraction may be available for operations at a particular time.

The number of available aircraft depends on maintenance, logistics, operational schedules and other factors.

### Why maintainability matters

Maintainability concerns how easily and quickly a system can be inspected, serviced, repaired or restored.

A system designed for maintainability may:

- Require less maintenance time.
- Allow easier access to components.
- Use modular assemblies.
- Simplify fault diagnosis.
- Reduce downtime.

This makes maintainability an important design consideration from the beginning of a military programme.

## 3.9 Defence aerospace business models

Defence companies may generate revenue through:

- Development contracts.
- Production contracts.
- Component supply.
- System integration.
- Upgrade programmes.
- Maintenance contracts.
- Training services.
- Technical support.
- Spare parts.
- Long-term sustainment agreements.

A company may continue receiving revenue from a military platform long after the original aircraft has been delivered.

# Day 4 — Space Industry: Launch Vehicles, Spacecraft and Satellite Systems

> **Objective:** Understand the architecture of the space industry, the distinction between launch vehicles and spacecraft, the space segment and ground segment, satellite services and the challenges of the orbital environment.

## 4.1 What makes the space industry different?

Space systems operate in an environment fundamentally different from Earth's atmosphere.

They may experience:

- Vacuum.
- Extreme temperature variations.
- Radiation.
- Micrometeoroids.
- Atomic oxygen in certain orbital environments.
- Long communication delays.
- Limited opportunities for physical repair.
- Orbital debris.

Unlike aircraft, most satellites cannot simply return to a maintenance facility for routine servicing.

Therefore, spacecraft must be designed to operate reliably with limited physical intervention.

## 4.2 The space industry architecture

The space industry can be divided into several connected segments.

### A. Launch segment

Responsible for transporting payloads from Earth into space.

It includes:

- Launch vehicles.
- Rocket engines.
- Launch facilities.
- Launch control systems.
- Range safety systems.
- Ground support equipment.

### B. Space segment

Consists of the spacecraft and other systems operating in space.

Examples:

- Satellites.
- Space stations.
- Space probes.
- Space telescopes.
- Orbital platforms.

### C. Ground segment

Consists of Earth-based infrastructure used to communicate with, control and receive information from spacecraft.

Examples:

- Ground stations.
- Antenna systems.
- Mission control centres.
- Tracking facilities.
- Data processing infrastructure.
- Satellite control software.

### D. User segment

Consists of the end users and systems that consume the services generated by space infrastructure.

Examples:

- Navigation receivers.
- Satellite communication terminals.
- Earth observation customers.
- Government agencies.
- Airlines.
- Ships.
- Mobile network providers.

These segments work together to deliver a complete space-based service.

## 4.3 Launch vehicles

A launch vehicle is a rocket-powered vehicle designed to transport payloads into a specified trajectory.

Its payload may be:

- A satellite.
- A spacecraft.
- A scientific instrument.
- Multiple satellites.
- Cargo for a space station.

### Main launch vehicle subsystems

**Propulsion system**

Generates the thrust needed to accelerate the vehicle.

**Structure**

Supports the vehicle's loads and maintains structural integrity.

**Guidance system**

Determines the vehicle's desired trajectory and guides it towards the intended path.

**Navigation system**

Estimates the vehicle's position, velocity and orientation.

**Control system**

Controls the vehicle's behaviour using actuators and propulsion mechanisms.

**Avionics**

Includes the electronic hardware and software required for vehicle operation.

**Payload fairing**

Protects the payload from aerodynamic and environmental loads during the atmospheric portion of launch.

### Multistage launch vehicles

Many launch vehicles use multiple stages.

Each stage contains propulsion and structural systems.

When a stage has completed its required operation, it may be separated from the vehicle.

Discarding empty or depleted stages reduces the mass that the remaining vehicle must accelerate.

This is one of the major reasons multistage launch vehicles are used.

### Important launch performance concepts

**Payload capacity**

The mass of payload that a launch vehicle can deliver to a specified orbit or trajectory.

**Specific impulse**

A measure of propulsion efficiency, commonly expressed in seconds.

**Thrust**

The force generated by the propulsion system.

**Delta-v**

The change in velocity that a vehicle or spacecraft can achieve.

**Orbital insertion**

The process of placing a spacecraft into its intended orbit.

Payload capacity is not a single universal number. It depends on the target orbit, trajectory, launch conditions and mission requirements.

## 4.4 Spacecraft and satellites

A spacecraft is a vehicle or system designed to operate in space.

A satellite is an object placed into orbit around a celestial body.

Satellites are therefore one major category of spacecraft.

### Major satellite subsystems

**Power subsystem**

Generates, stores and distributes electrical power.

Common technologies include:

- Solar panels.
- Batteries.
- Power conditioning electronics.

**Attitude determination and control system**

Determines and controls the spacecraft's orientation.

This is important because instruments and communication antennas must often point in specific directions.

**Communication subsystem**

Enables communication between the spacecraft and ground stations.

**Thermal control subsystem**

Maintains equipment within acceptable temperature limits.

**Onboard computer**

Processes commands, manages spacecraft operations and controls onboard functions.

**Propulsion subsystem**

Provides the capability to change or maintain orbital parameters or spacecraft orientation, depending on the mission.

**Payload**

The equipment that performs the satellite's primary mission.

For example, an Earth observation satellite may carry imaging instruments.

## 4.5 Types of satellites

### Communication satellites

Provide communication services.

Applications include:

- Television broadcasting.
- Broadband internet.
- Telecommunications.
- Remote connectivity.
- Government communication.

### Earth observation satellites

Collect information about Earth's surface and atmosphere.

Applications include:

- Agriculture.
- Weather monitoring.
- Disaster management.
- Environmental monitoring.
- Mapping.
- Infrastructure monitoring.

### Navigation satellites

Provide positioning, navigation and timing services.

Examples include:

- GPS.
- Galileo.
- GLONASS.
- BeiDou.
- NavIC.

### Meteorological satellites

Observe atmospheric and weather conditions.

Applications include:

- Weather forecasting.
- Cyclone monitoring.
- Cloud observation.
- Climate studies.

### Scientific satellites

Support research in astronomy, physics, planetary science and other scientific disciplines.

## 4.6 Orbital environment

The orbital environment has a major influence on spacecraft design.

### Vacuum

Spacecraft operate in an environment with extremely low surrounding pressure.

This affects:

- Lubrication.
- Material behaviour.
- Heat transfer.
- Gas release from materials.
- Component design.

### Thermal environment

Spacecraft may experience significant temperature changes depending on their orbit, orientation, shadow periods and exposure to solar radiation.

Thermal control systems use insulation, radiators, heaters and other methods to maintain acceptable temperatures.

### Radiation

Spacecraft electronics can be exposed to energetic particles and radiation.

This may cause:

- Single-event effects.
- Total ionising dose effects.
- Displacement damage.
- Electronic degradation.

Spacecraft may therefore use radiation-tolerant components, shielding and fault-tolerant designs.

### Micrometeoroids and orbital debris

Small particles and debris can travel at very high relative velocities.

Even small objects can damage spacecraft.

Designers must consider collision risk and suitable protection or avoidance strategies.

### Microgravity

Spacecraft in orbit experience apparent weightlessness because they and their contents are in continuous free fall around Earth.

This affects:

- Fluid behaviour.
- Human physiology.
- Thermal systems.
- Mechanical systems.
- Material processing.

## 4.7 Ground systems

Spacecraft cannot operate as completely isolated systems.

They require ground infrastructure.

### Ground stations

Ground stations use antennas and communication equipment to establish links with spacecraft.

They may be responsible for:

- Receiving telemetry.
- Sending commands.
- Tracking spacecraft.
- Receiving mission data.

### Mission control centres

Mission control centres monitor spacecraft health and coordinate mission operations.

They may manage:

- Spacecraft commands.
- Orbital information.
- Power status.
- Thermal status.
- Communication.
- Payload operations.
- Anomaly response.

### Telemetry, tracking and command

These are fundamental elements of spacecraft operations.

**Telemetry:** Information transmitted from a spacecraft to the ground about its status and performance.

**Tracking:** Determining the spacecraft's position and movement.

**Command:** Sending instructions from the ground to the spacecraft.

Together, these capabilities allow ground operators to monitor and control space missions.

## 4.8 Satellite services and commercial opportunities

A satellite is not always the final commercial product.

Often, the real business opportunity lies in the services generated by the satellite.

### Example: Earth observation

A company may operate imaging satellites and sell processed information to customers.

Potential customers include:

The commercial value may come from analysis rather than simply selling raw satellite images.

### Example: Satellite communication

- Maritime operators.

### Example: Navigation

Navigation satellites provide positioning, navigation and timing signals.

Commercial companies then develop receivers, software and services that use those signals.

# Day 5 — Regulations, Certification, Qualification and International Obligations

> **Objective:** Understand the different regulatory and approval frameworks governing civil aviation, defence aerospace and space activities, including international obligations.

## 5.1 Why does aerospace require regulation?

Aerospace systems operate in environments where failures can have significant consequences.

They may:

- Carry passengers.
- Operate in shared airspace.
- Perform military missions.
- Launch payloads into orbit.
- Generate radio-frequency interference.
- Create risks for other spacecraft.
- Affect national security.

Regulation establishes frameworks for safety, accountability, operational coordination and international responsibility.

However, the regulatory systems differ considerably across civil aviation, defence and space.

## 5.2 Civil aviation regulation

Civil aviation is governed by a mature international and national regulatory framework.

### ICAO

The International Civil Aviation Organization is a United Nations specialised agency.

It develops international Standards and Recommended Practices through its annexes to the Chicago Convention.

Its work covers areas such as:

- Personnel licensing.
- Rules of the air.
- Aircraft operations.
- Airworthiness.
- Aerodromes.
- Air traffic services.
- Accident investigation.
- Aviation security.

ICAO is not a worldwide aircraft certification authority.

National authorities are responsible for implementing and enforcing applicable requirements within their jurisdictions.

### National aviation authorities

Examples:

- FAA in the United States.
- EASA in the European Union.
- DGCA in India.
- UK CAA in the United Kingdom.

These authorities perform functions such as:

- Aircraft certification.
- Airworthiness oversight.
- Operator approvals.
- Maintenance organisation oversight.
- Personnel licensing.
- Aviation safety regulation.

### Chicago Convention

The Convention on International Civil Aviation, signed in 1944, established the foundation of the modern international civil aviation system.

It provides a framework for international cooperation and the regulation of civil aviation.

## 5.3 Defence qualification and regulatory frameworks

Defence aerospace systems are generally acquired according to military requirements and applicable national defence frameworks.

The military customer may establish technical requirements and qualification procedures.

These can involve:

- Environmental testing.
- Performance testing.
- Reliability testing.
- Interoperability assessment.
- Operational evaluation.
- Military airworthiness approval.
- Security evaluation.

### Military airworthiness

Military aircraft operate under national military airworthiness arrangements.

In India, the Centre for Military Airworthiness and Certification (CEMILAC), under DRDO, plays an important role in military airworthiness certification.

The Directorate General of Aeronautical Quality Assurance (DGAQA) is also an important organisation involved in quality assurance for military aerospace and defence equipment.

The exact responsibilities and approval processes depend on the programme and applicable framework.

### Standards and specifications

Military programmes may refer to standards covering:

- Environmental conditions.
- Electromagnetic compatibility.
- Materials.
- Manufacturing.
- Reliability.
- Testing.
- Quality assurance.

For example, the MIL-STD family contains United States military standards.

However, a military standard is not automatically a universal requirement for every defence programme.

Its applicability depends on the contract, procurement requirements and relevant authority.

## 5.4 Space regulation

Space activities involve both national regulation and international law.

Unlike civil aviation, there is no single global space regulator that issues all launch and satellite operating licences.

States regulate space activities through national legal frameworks while also carrying international obligations under space treaties.

### National space regulation

Countries establish legal and administrative frameworks for:

- Launch authorisation.
- Satellite operation.
- Space activities.
- Frequency use.
- Remote sensing.
- Launch facility operation.
- Liability and insurance requirements.
- Registration and supervision.

### India's space regulatory ecosystem

Important participants include:

**Department of Space (DoS)**

The government department responsible for India's space programme and related policy and institutional responsibilities.

**ISRO**

India's national space agency, responsible for major space research, development and mission activities.

**IN-SPACe**

The Indian National Space Promotion and Authorisation Centre.

It serves as an interface for promoting and authorising eligible non-government space activities in India under the applicable national framework.

**NSIL**

NewSpace India Limited.

A government-owned company involved in commercialising space technologies and services and supporting commercial space activities.

These organisations have different functions. They should not be treated as interchangeable.

## 5.5 International space law

Space activities involve international obligations because outer space is not treated as the territory of a state.

Several United Nations treaties form the foundation of international space law.

### A. Outer Space Treaty (1967)

The Treaty on Principles Governing the Activities of States in the Exploration and Use of Outer Space, including the Moon and Other Celestial Bodies.

Important principles include:

- Exploration and use of outer space for the benefit and interests of all countries.
- Freedom of exploration and use, subject to international law.
- No national appropriation of outer space.
- State responsibility for national space activities.
- International responsibility for damage caused by space objects.
- Restrictions concerning the placement of nuclear weapons and other weapons of mass destruction in orbit and the militarisation of celestial bodies as specified in the treaty.

The treaty does not prohibit every military activity in outer space.

Its specific provisions must be read carefully.

### B. Rescue Agreement (1968)

The Agreement on the Rescue of Astronauts, the Return of Astronauts and the Return of Objects Launched into Outer Space.

It establishes obligations concerning assistance to astronauts and the return of certain space objects under specified circumstances.

### C. Liability Convention (1972)

The Convention on International Liability for Damage Caused by Space Objects.

It establishes an international framework for liability for damage caused by space objects.

Broadly:

- A launching state is absolutely liable for damage caused on the surface of the Earth or to aircraft in flight.
- Liability for damage caused elsewhere than on the Earth's surface is based on fault under the convention.

### D. Registration Convention (1975)

The Convention on Registration of Objects Launched into Outer Space.

It establishes registration-related obligations for space objects.

Registration helps identify objects launched into space and supports transparency and accountability.

### E. Moon Agreement (1979)

The Agreement Governing the Activities of States on the Moon and Other Celestial Bodies.

It addresses activities on the Moon and other celestial bodies.

Its participation is much more limited than that of the Outer Space Treaty.

### Important concept: Launching state

A launching state is a state that launches or procures the launching of a space object, or from whose territory or facility a space object is launched, according to the definitions in the Liability Convention and Registration Convention.

More than one state may qualify as a launching state for a particular space object.

## 5.6 International frequency and orbital coordination

Satellites rely on radio-frequency communication.

However, radio-frequency spectrum is a limited resource.

If different satellite systems transmit without coordination, interference can affect communications.

The International Telecommunication Union (ITU) coordinates the international use of radio-frequency spectrum and satellite orbit resources through its regulatory framework.

Important functions include:

- Frequency allocation.
- Satellite network coordination.
- International notification.
- Registration of frequency assignments.
- Interference management.

The ITU is particularly important for satellite communications and broadcasting.

### Why does this matter?

A company may successfully manufacture a satellite and launch it into orbit.

However, it still needs to operate its communication systems within the applicable spectrum and coordination framework.

Technical capability alone does not establish permission to use every frequency or orbital resource.

## 5.7 Launch regulation and licensing

Launching a rocket involves significant safety, technical and legal considerations.

A launch may require authorisation relating to:

- Launch vehicle operations.
- Launch site operations.
- Public safety.
- Range safety.
- Payload.
- Environmental considerations.
- Insurance.
- Liability.
- National security.
- Radio-frequency use.

The exact licensing framework depends on the country and the nature of the activity.

For example, the United States uses a commercial launch licensing framework administered by the Federal Aviation Administration.

India has its own national framework for authorising space activities, including the role of IN-SPACe.

### Launch authorisation versus spacecraft certification

These should not be confused.

Launch authorisation concerns permission to conduct the launch activity under the applicable legal and regulatory framework.

Spacecraft testing and qualification concern whether the spacecraft satisfies the technical requirements for its intended mission.

One does not automatically replace the other.

## 5.8 International obligations and state responsibility

A major difference between space and ordinary commercial manufacturing is the role of states in international responsibility.

Under the Outer Space Treaty, states bear international responsibility for national space activities, including activities conducted by non-governmental entities.

Non-governmental space activities require authorisation and continuing supervision by the appropriate state.

This means private companies do not operate outside international space law simply because they are privately owned.

National governments have responsibilities relating to the activities they authorise.

## 5.9 Space debris and responsible operations

Space debris refers to non-functional human-made objects in orbit or fragments of such objects.

Examples include:

- Defunct satellites.
- Spent rocket stages.
- Fragmentation debris.
- Mission-related objects.

Space debris creates collision risks for operational spacecraft.

### Why is debris management important?

A collision can generate additional fragments.

These fragments may collide with other objects and create further debris.

This can increase the long-term risk to space operations.

### Mitigation measures

- End-of-life disposal planning.
- Passivation of spacecraft and rocket stages.
- Collision avoidance.
- Responsible mission design.
- Debris monitoring.
- Deorbiting where appropriate.
- Limiting unnecessary release of objects.

Space debris mitigation guidelines and technical standards are important, but not every guideline is itself a binding international treaty obligation.

# Day 6 — Business Models, Economics and Product Lifecycles

> **Objective:** Understand how civil aviation, defence aerospace and the space industry generate revenue, manage costs, acquire customers and support products throughout their operational lifecycles.

## 6.1 Why economics matters in aerospace

Aerospace engineering does not operate independently of economics.

An aircraft may be technically excellent but commercially unsuccessful if its operating costs are too high.

A defence system may satisfy technical requirements but become difficult to acquire if its programme cost exceeds the available budget.

A satellite may successfully operate in orbit but fail as a business if there are insufficient customers for its services.

Engineering decisions therefore influence:

- Manufacturing costs.
- Development expenditure.
- Operating costs.
- Maintenance requirements.
- Product pricing.
- Customer demand.
- Commercial competitiveness.

## 6.2 Civil aviation business model

Civil aviation primarily operates through commercial revenue.

### Aircraft OEM revenue

Aircraft manufacturers may earn revenue through:

- Aircraft sales.
- Spare parts.
- Maintenance support.
- Engineering services.
- Training.
- Upgrades.
- Technical support.

### Airline revenue

Airlines generate revenue through:

- Passenger tickets.
- Cargo transportation.
- Ancillary services.
- Loyalty programmes.
- Additional commercial services.

### Aircraft leasing

Aircraft leasing companies purchase aircraft and lease them to airlines.

This allows airlines to operate aircraft without necessarily purchasing them outright.

Leasing can reduce upfront capital requirements, although it creates ongoing financial obligations.

### MRO revenue

MRO organisations generate revenue through:

- Scheduled maintenance.
- Engine overhaul.
- Component repair.
- Inspections.
- Spare parts.
- Fleet support contracts.

### Important commercial metrics

**ASK — Available Seat Kilometres**

A measure of available passenger capacity.

$$
ASK=\text{Available Seats}\times\text{Distance Flown}
$$

**RPK — Revenue Passenger Kilometres**

A measure of passenger traffic.

$$
RPK=\text{Revenue Passengers}\times\text{Distance Flown}
$$

**Load Factor**

$$
\text{Load Factor}=\frac{RPK}{ASK}\times100
$$

**Aircraft utilisation**

A measure of how intensively an aircraft is used, often expressed in flight hours or cycles over a given period.

These metrics help airlines evaluate operational efficiency and commercial performance.

## 6.3 Defence aerospace business model

### Development contracts

A defence company may depend on a relatively small number of large government customers.

This creates both opportunities and risks.

The initial purchase price does not represent the total cost of owning and operating an aerospace product.

For a satellite, costs may include launch, ground operations, data processing, communication, insurance and end-of-life disposal.

Engineers must consider lifecycle economics because design decisions made early can significantly influence future costs.

## Final Learning Objective

Do not conclude that civil aviation is simply about safety, defence is simply about weapons and space is simply about rockets.

Each sector is a complex industrial ecosystem with its own engineering objectives, customers, commercial structures, regulatory requirements and strategic dependencies.

The real objective is to understand **how the intended mission determines the engineering, business and regulatory architecture of an aerospace product.**

By the end of Week 2, you should be able to look at any aerospace company or product and determine:

1. Which sector does it belong to?
2. What problem does it solve?
3. Who pays for it?
4. Who operates or uses it?
5. What technical requirements must it satisfy?
6. Which regulatory or approval framework applies?
7. How does it generate economic or strategic value?
8. What happens after the product is delivered?
