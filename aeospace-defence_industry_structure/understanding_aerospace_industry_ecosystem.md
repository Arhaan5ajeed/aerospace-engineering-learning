## Week Overview

### What are we studying?

The aerospace industry is not simply a collection of companies that manufacture aircraft.

It is a complex industrial ecosystem involving:

- Engineering and design.
- Raw material extraction and processing.
- Component manufacturing.
- Engine and propulsion development.
- Avionics and electronics.
- Software and computing.
- Aircraft and spacecraft integration.
- Testing and certification.
- Military and civilian procurement.
- Commercial operations.
- Maintenance, repair and overhaul.
- Research and technological development.

No single organisation independently possesses all the resources, expertise, infrastructure and capabilities required to develop and operate a modern aircraft.

Instead, organisations specialise in particular activities and cooperate through interconnected supply chains.

### The two major ecosystems

We will study two interconnected structures.

#### 1. The commercial and industrial supply chain

The simplified structure is:

**Raw Material Suppliers → Tier-3 Suppliers → Tier-2 Suppliers → Tier-1 Suppliers → OEM**

This represents the movement of materials, components, assemblies and systems towards the organisation responsible for delivering the final product.

#### 2. The defence procurement ecosystem

The simplified structure is:

**Government → Military → Procurement Authority → Prime Contractor → Suppliers**

This represents the movement of requirements, funding, contracts and accountability.

The first structure primarily describes industrial production. The second describes how governments identify requirements, purchase military capabilities and manage defence programmes.

They frequently intersect, but should not be treated as identical.

## Day 1 — Mapping the Aerospace Industry

> **Objective:** Understand the aerospace industry as a complete ecosystem and identify the major categories of organisations participating in it.

### 1.1 What is the aerospace industry?

The aerospace industry encompasses the research, design, development, manufacturing, operation and maintenance of vehicles and systems that operate within Earth's atmosphere or outer space.

It broadly consists of two sectors.

#### A. Aeronautics

Aeronautics concerns the science, engineering and technology of flight within Earth's atmosphere.

Examples include:

- Commercial passenger aircraft.
- Military fighter aircraft.
- Transport aircraft.
- Helicopters.
- Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs).
- Business jets.
- Airships.
- General aviation aircraft.

#### B. Astronautics

Astronautics concerns the science, engineering and technology of vehicles and systems operating beyond Earth's atmosphere.

Examples include:

- Launch vehicles.
- Satellites.
- Spacecraft.
- Space stations.
- Space probes.
- Reusable launch systems.
- Space-based communication infrastructure.

Although these sectors have different technical requirements, they share several industrial capabilities, including propulsion, materials, electronics, software, control systems and systems engineering.

### 1.2 The major participants

The aerospace ecosystem can be divided into several categories.

| Participant | Primary responsibility |
|---|---|
| OEMs | Develop and deliver complete aircraft or aerospace products |
| Tier-1 suppliers | Supply major systems and assemblies |
| Tier-2 suppliers | Supply subsystems, components and specialised assemblies |
| Tier-3 suppliers | Supply lower-level components, parts and manufacturing services |
| Component manufacturers | Manufacture individual mechanical, electrical and electronic components |
| Engine manufacturers | Develop and manufacture propulsion systems |
| Avionics companies | Develop aircraft electronic systems |
| Software companies | Develop software for engineering, operations and aerospace applications |
| System integrators | Combine subsystems into functioning systems or platforms |
| MROs | Maintain, repair and overhaul aerospace equipment |
| Airlines | Operate commercial passenger and cargo aircraft |
| Airports | Provide infrastructure for aircraft operations |
| Regulators | Establish and enforce aviation safety and certification requirements |
| Militaries | Define, acquire and operate military aerospace capabilities |
| Research organisations | Conduct scientific and technological research |
| Universities | Educate engineers and conduct research |
| Startups | Develop new technologies, products and business models |

These categories are not necessarily mutually exclusive. An organisation may occupy several roles simultaneously.

For example, a company may manufacture components, provide maintenance services and develop engineering software.

### 1.3 How the ecosystem creates value

Consider a commercial passenger aircraft.

Before an airline can transport passengers, an extensive industrial ecosystem must function.

1. Material suppliers provide aluminium alloys, titanium, composites and other materials.
2. Component manufacturers produce fasteners, bearings, actuators, sensors and electrical components.
3. Tier-2 suppliers manufacture specialised assemblies.
4. Tier-1 suppliers provide major systems such as landing gear, avionics and environmental control systems.
5. The OEM designs and integrates the aircraft.
6. Regulators establish the certification requirements and approve the aircraft for operation.
7. Airlines purchase or lease the aircraft.
8. Airports provide the infrastructure necessary for aircraft operations.
9. MRO organisations maintain the aircraft throughout its operational life.

Each participant contributes a different capability.

The aircraft is therefore the result of an interconnected industrial system rather than the output of one isolated manufacturer.

### 1.4 The aerospace industry is interconnected

Aerospace organisations do not operate independently.

Consider the relationship between an aircraft manufacturer and an engine manufacturer.

An aircraft OEM may design the fuselage, wings, flight controls and aircraft architecture while sourcing engines from specialised propulsion companies.

The engine manufacturer may independently develop the engine core, combustion system, turbine stages and control systems.

Both organisations must cooperate to ensure that the engine can be integrated safely and efficiently into the aircraft.

This creates technological and commercial interdependence.

Other examples include:

- An avionics manufacturer depending on semiconductor suppliers.
- A composite manufacturer depending on specialised chemical suppliers.
- An aircraft manufacturer depending on software used for engineering simulation.
- An airline depending on MRO organisations for aircraft maintenance.
- A defence contractor depending on government contracts.

### 1.5 Civilian and military aerospace

The aerospace industry also has two major customer environments.

#### Civilian aerospace

Civilian aerospace serves commercial and non-military requirements.

Examples:

- Passenger transportation.
- Cargo transportation.
- Business aviation.
- Civil helicopters.
- Weather monitoring.
- Earth observation.
- Satellite communications.
- Space transportation.

Customers include airlines, aircraft leasing companies, private operators, governments and commercial satellite operators.

#### Military aerospace

Military aerospace develops and supplies capabilities for national defence and security.

Examples:

- Fighter aircraft.
- Military transport aircraft.
- Helicopters.
- UAVs.
- Missiles.
- Radar systems.
- Electronic warfare systems.
- Military communications.
- Surveillance and reconnaissance systems.

Customers include defence ministries, armed forces and government procurement organisations.

#### Important distinction

Aerospace companies are not necessarily restricted to one sector.

Some organisations develop both civilian and military products. Others specialise in one market.

A company's position in the aerospace ecosystem depends on its products, customers, capabilities and contractual responsibilities.
